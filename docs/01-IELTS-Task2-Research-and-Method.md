# IELTS Academic Writing Task 2 — Research Report and Method

Prepared for T.Chris (Satriwithaya School EP) as the evidence base for **Position Control**, the Task 2 sibling of Chart Control. Cohorts: M4.1 (30 students, A2–B2, one period a week plus homework) and the private tutees (A2–B1, 12–17). Target: Band 7 for the class track, Band 8 for the tutoring track.

---

## 1. What the test actually asks

Task 2 is a 40-minute essay of at least 250 words on a given point of view, argument or problem. The official format page states that the task assesses the ability to build "a clear, relevant, well-organised argument, giving evidence or examples to support your ideas, and use language accurately", that Task 2 "contributes twice as much as Task 1 to the Writing score", and that candidates are penalised for going off topic, for not writing "a whole piece of connected text (i.e. you must not use notes or bullet points)", and "severely" for plagiarised text. Under-length answers are not penalised by a fixed deduction; rather "there may not be enough evidence of the language features needed in order to award higher bands" ([ielts.org — Academic Writing format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-writing)).

Four criteria carry equal weight (25% each): Task Response (TR), Coherence and Cohesion (CC), Lexical Resource (LR), Grammatical Range and Accuracy (GRA). The reported band is their average.

Arithmetic the students must know cold: Task 2 is two-thirds of the Writing score; 40 minutes; 250 words minimum (aim 260–290); four criteria at 25% each; one position, held from the first paragraph to the last.

## 2. The band descriptors as gates

The public descriptors (May 2023 wording, [ielts.org PDF](https://ielts.org/cdn/Guides/ielts-writing-band-descriptors.pdf)) are written so that each band names a specific thing the essay must do. Reading them as *gates* — the single change that lifts a criterion half a band — is the method behind every module in the app.

**Task Response.** Band 6: "the main parts of the prompt are addressed … a position is presented that is directly relevant to the prompt, although the conclusions drawn may be unclear, unjustified or repetitive." Band 7: "a clear and developed position is presented. Main ideas are extended and supported but there may be a tendency to over-generalise or there may be a lack of focus and precision in supporting ideas/material." Band 8: "the prompt is appropriately and sufficiently addressed. A clear and well-developed position … Ideas are relevant, well extended and supported." The 6→7 gate is a *clear position throughout*; the 7→8 gate is *no over-generalisation*: every main idea carries a mechanism (how it works) and something concrete (an example or a specific consequence). That is precisely what the Facet → Mechanism → Example → Nuance chain of Chris's 11-variable template forces a student to produce.

**Coherence and Cohesion.** Band 6: cohesion "may be faulty or mechanical due to misuse, overuse or omission." Band 7: "logically organised … clear progression throughout … a range of cohesive devices including reference and substitution is used flexibly but with some inaccuracies or some over/under use"; one clear central topic per paragraph. Band 8: "the message can be followed with ease … cohesion is well managed." Band 9: cohesion "very rarely attracts attention." The 6→7 gate is one idea per paragraph with visible progression; the 7→8 gate is *invisible cohesion* — reference (this trend, such measures, doing so), substitution and known-to-new sentence chaining instead of Firstly / Moreover / In conclusion on every sentence.

**Lexical Resource.** Band 6: "generally adequate … a rather restricted range or a lack of precision." Band 7: "sufficient to allow some flexibility and precision … some ability to use less common and/or idiomatic items. An awareness of style and collocation is evident, though inappropriacies occur." Band 8: "a wide resource is fluently and flexibly used to convey precise meanings." The 7→8 gate is *collocation precision*, not rarity: a mid-frequency academic word in its correct partner phrase (mitigate climate change, act as a deterrent, allocate public funds) outscores an obscure synonym used slightly wrongly, which actively lowers the mark.

**Grammatical Range and Accuracy.** Band 6: "a mix of simple and complex sentence forms … complex structures are not marked by the same level of accuracy as … simple structures." Band 7: "a variety of complex structures is used with some flexibility and accuracy … error-free sentences are frequent." Band 8: "a wide range of structures is flexibly and accurately used. The majority of sentences are error-free." The 7→8 gate is *error-free sentence density*. Corpus work on academic writing (Biber, Gray & Poonpon 2011) shows that advanced complexity is phrasal — dense noun phrases and nominalisation — rather than stacked subordinate clauses, so the highest-yield grammar drill is turning clauses into noun phrases ("Because prices rose, people bought less" → "Rising prices reduced consumption"), and the safest range strategy is three or four structures mastered to near-perfect accuracy.

Two further descriptor facts matter for this cohort. Bands 3–4 mention "possible over-dependence on input material or memorised language", and a fully memorised essay is Band 0; a response of 20 words or fewer is Band 1. Memorised language is therefore a real, named risk for any template-based method (see §5).

## 3. Question types and the traps in each

There are five typologies. No IELTS partner publishes frequencies, so all five must be taught, but two-sided prompts (discuss both views; agree/disagree) dominate practice materials and Chris's existing bank.

| Type | Signal | What TR demands | The trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opinion (agree/disagree) | "To what extent do you agree or disagree?" | One position, sustained, with reasons | Sitting on the fence; "partly agree" without saying which part |
| Discuss both views + opinion | "Discuss both views and give your own opinion." | Fair treatment of both, then a stated verdict | Opinion appears only in the conclusion, or never |
| Advantages / disadvantages | "Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?" | A *weighing* — this is an opinion question | Listing pros and cons with no verdict |
| Problem / cause / solution | "What are the causes? What can be done?" | Causes named, each solution mapped to a cause | Solutions that do not answer the named causes |
| Two-part / direct questions | Two separate questions | Both answered with equal weight | One question answered in a sentence |

The IDP examiner article on the seven mistakes that stop Band 7 lists exactly these failures: partial answers, an unclear position, inadequate paragraphing, non-essay formats, memorised clichés ("double-edged sword", "in a nutshell"), unverifiable "research shows" claims, and spelling ([IDP — 7 mistakes](https://ielts.idp.com/prepare/article-ielts-writing-task-2-7-mistakes-preventing-a-band-7)). Its advice to ground examples in the writer's own country, school or family is directly usable for Thai teenagers: a Bangkok example is worth more than an invented statistic.

## 4. Planning: the 5 / 30 / 5 protocol

Practitioner consensus (IELTS Advantage, IELTS Jacky and others) is a three-stage protocol: five minutes to decode the question and plan, thirty to write, five to check. Two findings from that literature shape the app. First, "there are no extra points for sophisticated or complex ideas": the best ideas are the obvious, relevant ones that can be *developed* in four sentences ([IELTS Advantage strategy](https://www.ieltsadvantage.com/ielts-writing-task-2-strategy/)). Second, ideas come faster through a fixed lens than through free brainstorming — a stakeholder scan (individuals, families, business, government, future generations) or a SPEEC scan (social, political, economic, environmental, cultural), and the same five argument schemas recur in every domain: individual vs collective responsibility, short-term cost vs long-term benefit, freedom vs regulation, tradition vs progress, prevention vs cure.

Within the plan, every body paragraph follows the same four moves: topic sentence (the facet), mechanism (how it works), example (where it happens), nuance (the limit that proves the writer can think). This is the shape of a Band 8 paragraph in the descriptors' own terms — "extended and supported", not "over-generalised".

## 5. The 11-Variable Matrix template — and how to keep it legal

Chris's "Modular Lexical Matrix" (the IELTS Writing Task 2 Template deck, and the Matrix Arena game at IELTS-T2-W-P2) reduces a two-sided essay to eleven decisions:

1. Core Topic (a nominalised noun phrase)
2. Facet A · 3. Mechanism A · 4. Example A · 5. Nuance A
6. Facet B · 7. Mechanism B · 8. Example B · 9. Nuance B
10. Synthesized Position · 11. Rationale

and supplies paragraph frames at two tiers. The B2 tier ("B2 Essay Engineering: Blueprint to Assembly"):

- P1: *People often discuss the topic of [Core Topic] because it has many different effects. While many people focus on [Facet A], it is also important to consider [Facet B]. To fully understand this issue, we need to look at both sides carefully.*
- P2: *One important part of this issue is [Facet A]. This idea works by [Mechanism A]. We can see this happening in the real world when [Example A]. However, looking only at this side misses an important point; in reality, [Nuance A], which shows that this is not a perfect solution.*
- P3: *Another very important part of the debate is [Facet B]. This side is based on [Mechanism B], which usually leads to results like [Example B]. Still, we must also be careful with this view. Even though it has a big impact, we must remember that [Nuance B].*
- P4: *To sum up, simply looking at [Core Topic] from one side is not enough to show the whole picture. A better way to look at it is that [Synthesized Position]. This view makes the most sense because [Rationale].*

The C1 tier ("AR Lexical Matrix") offers three openers per paragraph (Standard / Nominalised / Action-oriented for P1; Standard / Causality / Argumentative for P2; Standard / Parallelism / Complication for P3; Standard / Perspective / Evaluative for P4), two nuance alternates, and three "structural swaps" — inversion (*Seldom does this approach account for [Nuance]*), cleft sentences (*It is within real-world frameworks, such as [Example], that this trajectory most clearly manifests*), and participle phrases (*… operates primarily by [Mechanism], subsequently triggering [Example]*) — plus the Lens method (economic, socio-cultural, public-health lenses) to make every student's variables different.

Why it works against the descriptors: the eleven variables *are* the Band 8 TR requirements (position, extension, support, nuance) made explicit; the frames guarantee paragraphing, progression and one idea per paragraph (CC 7); the nominalised Core Topic and the swaps are the phrasal complexity that drives GRA 8; the Lens method forces precise, domain-specific collocation (LR 7→8).

Why it is dangerous if used naively: the frame text is *memorised language*. An examiner who reads "A fundamental dimension of this issue is its relationship to" in ten scripts from one school will discount it, and the descriptors say so. Three design rules therefore govern the app:

1. **The template is a scaffold that fades.** Guided mode shows full frames; Skeleton mode shows only the slot labels and word targets; Exam mode shows a blank page. Students earn their way down the ladder, and mocks are always blank-page.
2. **The frames are customisable, not fixed.** Every paragraph has at least three openers, every student is expected to edit the frame into their own words, and the app stores "my template" per student. A student who ships the same frame verbatim in every essay is flagged by the pre-flight check (*template ratio*).
3. **The variables carry the marks, not the frames.** Most of the word count in a Band 8 essay is inside the slots — the mechanism, the example and the nuance — so a student who fills the slots richly and writes the frames plainly still scores; a student who polishes frames and leaves the slots thin does not.

For the other three typologies the same eleven variables are re-mapped rather than replaced (the "playbooks"): for an opinion essay Facet A and B are two *reasons* for one position (or a reason and a conceded counter-argument); for advantages/disadvantages they are the advantage and the disadvantage and the Synthesized Position is the verdict on which outweighs; for problem/solution Facet A is the cause with its mechanism and Facet B the solution that answers it; for two-part questions each body paragraph answers one question.

## 6. What the classroom evidence says about this cohort

The M4.1 in-class exam (a foreign-language-from-early-grades prompt, 40 minutes, handwritten) marked through LevelUp showed the familiar B1–B2 profile: positions that appear only in the conclusion, body paragraphs with a claim and no mechanism, "Firstly / Secondly / In conclusion" as the only cohesion, articles and subject–verb agreement as the visible grammar faults, and word counts clustered around the 250 line. The Matrix Arena game showed that students can select the right Core Topic and Facets from options far faster than they can generate them, which is the argument for a Bootcamp that walks the eleven decisions as drop-downs before any writing is asked for.

## 7. The method, in the order the app teaches it

1. **The Test** — the facts and the four gates.
2. **Decode** — the five question types and what each demands; instruction words; the "outweigh" trap.
3. **Position** — one-sentence thesis, held in the introduction and the conclusion.
4. **Matrix I** — Core Topic (nominalised) and the two Facets; near-miss distractors from the Arena bank.
5. **Matrix II** — Mechanism, Example, Nuance: de-generalise every claim.
6. **The Template** — the four frames at two tiers; choosing and editing an opener.
7. **Synthesis** — Synthesized Position and Rationale; evaluate, do not summarise; no new ideas.
8. **Cohesion** — reference, substitution, known-to-new; the linker detox.
9. **Nominalisation** — clauses into noun phrases; the Band 8 driver.
10. **Structural Swaps** — inversion, cleft, participle, concession, conditionals; three or four to 90% accuracy before adding a fifth.
11. **Collocation and Register** — domain collocations; precision over rarity; the cliché ban.
12. **Accuracy** — articles, agreement, plurals, punctuation, word form: the Thai-learner error families.
13. **Playbooks** — re-mapping the matrix to the other four question types.
14. **Exam Day** — 5/30/5; the ten-point pre-flight check; proofreading one's own top three errors.

## 8. The ten-point pre-flight check (student-facing)

1. Four or five paragraphs, and the position is stated in paragraph one.
2. The position is restated (not merely repeated) in the last paragraph, with no new idea.
3. Every part of the question is answered; both views appear if the prompt names two.
4. Each body paragraph has one central idea, a mechanism and a concrete example.
5. Each body paragraph admits a limit (nuance) without changing the position.
6. Cohesion by reference and substitution; no more than one visible linker per paragraph opening.
7. No memorised clichés, no "research shows", no "I think" in every sentence.
8. Formal register: no contractions, no rhetorical questions, no bullet points.
9. Complex structures used are ones the writer controls; one deliberate swap per body paragraph at most.
10. 260–290 words.
