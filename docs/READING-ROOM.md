# The Reading Room — Harry Potter scenes for Quillmoor Academy (teacher only)

**Who this is for:** T.Chris, for the five Tutoring tutees (Aeh, Mikii, Donut, JingJing, Pear; B1/B2, target Band 7.5).
**IP level: C.** Quillmoor Academy (the app) is an original world and never names Harry Potter, Hogwarts, the houses, spells or characters. The real scenes live only here, in class discussion and in what you say aloud. Nothing on this page goes into the app, the GitHub Pages site or a public slide.

**How to read this page.** Each chapter of the app has one canon scene that mirrors its skill. The scene is the warm-up and the talking point; the IELTS prompt is always real-world. Book numbers and chapter titles are the UK Bloomsbury editions; the Thai Nanmeebooks editions keep the same chapter order. Where the film differs from the book, the book wins and the difference is noted. Summaries are in my own words; at most one short line is quoted per scene.

**Why Harry Potter stays outside the app.** The tutees read on a public site. Rowling's names and Warner Bros.' marks are enforced when used as branding or copied at length; a short scene discussed in a private lesson is ordinary teaching use. Keep it that way: discuss, quote one line, never paste chapters into a shared doc.

## Chapter 1 — The Letter · Hall of Lanterns

**App skill:** the numbers of the test (40 minutes, 250+ words, four examiners weighted equally) and decoding the five instruction types. The Sorting Lantern finds her strongest criterion.

**Scene:** *Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone* (book 1), chapter 7, "The Sorting Hat".
The first-years wait in plain black robes, still unsorted. The Hat sings a song that says plainly what each house looks for: bravery, hard work and loyalty, learning, ambition. Then it reads each child in turn. On Harry's head it considers Slytherin, and Harry thinks "Not Slytherin" over and over; the Hat says he could be great there, then sends him to Gryffindor.
*One line:* "Not Slytherin, eh?"

**Why it fits:** the Hat's song is the band descriptors read aloud: before anyone is judged, the judge tells you exactly what it is looking for. The Lantern does the same with the four criteria. And the Hat sorts by what you already are, not by what you wish you were — which is why the Sorting questions ask for real English, not for a favourite colour.

**Discussion (B1–B2):**
1. The Hat tells the students what each house values before it sorts them. Why is that fair? What would be unfair?
2. Harry chooses "not Slytherin". Can a person choose her strength, or only discover it? Give one example from your own school life.
3. The Lantern has four houses: direction, order, the right word, control. Which one do you think is yours, and what makes you say so?

**IELTS Task 2 prompt (OPINION):** "In some countries, children are placed in different classes according to their ability from the age of seven or eight. To what extent do you agree or disagree with this practice?"
Decode first: one claim, one position, two reasons (or one reason and one conceded limit).

**2-minute warm-up — "The song before the sorting":** read the student the four Quillmoor house mottos (Know where you stand · Every stone carries the next · The right word, not the rare word · No loose threads). She has 60 seconds to match each motto to one of TR, CC, LR, GRA and say in one sentence what that examiner counts. Then she takes the Sorting.

## Chapter 2 — The First Duel · Debating Chamber

**App skill:** the position: one sentence the examiner can quote back (marker, claim, reason, limit; 15–30 words), stated in paragraph one and held to the end. The Chamber Campaign tests whether she can hold it for a week.

**Scene:** *Harry Potter and the Goblet of Fire* (book 4), chapter 14, "The Unforgivable Curses".
After seeing how the house-elf Winky is treated, Hermione founds S.P.E.W. (the Society for the Promotion of Elfish Welfare) and shows Harry and Ron the badges. Ron laughs at the name and says the elves like working; later Hagrid says the same, that freeing them would be unkind. Hermione does not move. She keeps her position for the rest of the book and beyond, even when nobody joins.
(Film note: S.P.E.W. is cut from the films entirely; use the book.)

**Why it fits:** Hermione has a position she can state in one sentence, a reason, and a limit she admits (most elves do not want to be freed) without abandoning the claim. Ron is the voice of "everybody knows", the Gossip Quill's voice. The Chamber Campaign in the app is the same test: pin a sentence on Monday, hold it to Friday.

**Discussion (B1–B2):**
1. Say Hermione's position in one sentence of your own, with a reason. Can your partner quote it back?
2. Ron says "the elves like it". Is that a reason, or a limit? How could Hermione admit it and still keep her position?
3. Hermione keeps her view when everyone laughs. When is that strength, and when is it stubbornness?

**IELTS Task 2 prompt (DISCUSS):** "Some people believe that unpaid internships give young people valuable experience and should be encouraged. Others argue that they are a form of exploitation that only wealthy families can afford. Discuss both views and give your own opinion."
Decode first: both views need a mechanism and an example; the verdict goes in paragraph one **and** the conclusion.

**2-minute warm-up — "Quote it back":** she states her position on the prompt aloud in one sentence (marker, claim, reason, limit). You repeat it back word for word. If you cannot, it is not a position yet; she tries again. Two attempts, then into the Scriptorium.

## Chapter 3 — The Living Map · Map Room

**App skill:** the eleven keys of the Matrix — Core Topic as a noun phrase, two facets, and for each a mechanism (how), an example (where) and a nuance (the limit that keeps the position). Felix's question: "Where's the example?"

**Scene:** *Harry Potter and the Prisoner of Azkaban* (book 3), chapter 10, "The Marauder's Map".
Harry cannot go to Hogsmeade without a signed form. Fred and George give him a blank piece of parchment and show him how it works: tap it, say the password, and a map of the castle appears with every person's name moving on it, plus seven secret passages. They point him to one: the hump of the one-eyed witch statue leads straight to the cellar of Honeydukes.
*One line:* "I solemnly swear that I am up to no good."

**Why it fits:** the twins make a claim (this map is worth more than anything), explain the mechanism (it shows where everyone is, so you can see Filch coming), and give a concrete example (the one-eyed witch passage to Honeydukes). Claim → how → where is exactly one body paragraph. And the map draws itself only when you say the right words — the Living Map in the app does the same with the eleven keys.

**Discussion (B1–B2):**
1. Fred and George do not just say "the map is great". What do they explain, and what do they show? Which part convinces Harry most?
2. The map shows where everyone is, all the time. Who would like that, and who would hate it? (This is your two facets.)
3. Harry uses the map to break a rule. Give one limit of the map's "advantage" that does not change your opinion of it.

**IELTS Task 2 prompt (ADVANTAGE):** "Schools and city councils increasingly use cameras and location tracking to keep students and citizens safe. Do the advantages of this development outweigh the disadvantages?"
Decode first: a weighing, not a list; say which side is heavier and why.

**2-minute warm-up — "Point at it":** you give a claim from the prompt ("Cameras in school corridors reduce bullying"). She has 30 seconds for the *how* (one sentence with "because" or "so") and 30 seconds for the *where* (a real place, policy or practice she can name). If she says "research shows", the clock restarts.

## Chapter 4 — The Spellbook · Spellbook Workshop

**App skill:** her personalised template — four frames, eleven slots, her own lines (Template Lab share 60/50/40/30), the Ten Wards, and the first essay sent to the examiners. Celeste's copied page fades because it is not hers.

**Scene:** *Harry Potter and the Half-Blood Prince* (book 6), chapter 9, "The Half-Blood Prince".
In Slughorn's first Potions lesson Harry has no book, so he is lent an old, battered copy of *Advanced Potion-Making*. Someone has written all over it: shortcuts, corrections, better methods. Following those notes instead of the printed text, Harry brews the best Draught of Living Death in the class and wins the bottle of Felix Felicis. The cover says the book belongs to "the Half-Blood Prince". Hermione, who followed the printed instructions exactly, is furious.
(Later in the book Harry casts a curse from the same notes without understanding it and badly hurts someone; with the tutees, say only that the borrowed notes eventually led him somewhere he could not control.)

**Why it fits:** borrowed words work until they don't. Harry gets a brilliant result from somebody else's notes, but he does not understand why the method works, and when the notes run out he is lost. That is Celeste with the grey page. A spellbook is only safe when the lines are yours and you know what each slot does — which is why the Workshop makes her read her frames aloud.

**Discussion (B1–B2):**
1. Harry wins the prize using the Prince's notes. Did he deserve it? Say yes or no, then give one reason and one limit.
2. Hermione followed the printed book and lost. What is the difference between following instructions and understanding them?
3. If you had the Prince's book, what would you copy and what would you change? How is that like building your own spellbook?

**IELTS Task 2 prompt (OPINION):** "Some teachers believe that students who use AI tools to help write their homework are cheating. To what extent do you agree or disagree?"
Decode first: one claim to agree or disagree with; "partly agree" only if she names which part.

**2-minute warm-up — "Whose line is it?":** show her two introduction sentences: one from a published generic frame ("In today's modern world, this topic is a hot topic"), one that she wrote in the Workshop. She says which is hers, why she can tell, and which one the examiner has read thirty times today. Then she reads her own first frame aloud and keeps it only if it sounds like her.

## Chapter 5 — The Potions Lab · Brewing Cellars

**App skill:** cohesion that the reader cannot see — reference and substitution ("this charge", "such a ban", "doing so") before linkers; known-first, new-last; nominalisation; three or four complex structures controlled to about 90% before adding a fifth. The Whisper boiled: linker soup on top, nothing underneath.

**Scene:** *Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone* (book 1), chapter 8, "The Potions Master".
Snape's first lesson in the dungeon. He opens with a short, exact speech about what Potions can do, then fires three questions at Harry: what asphodel and wormwood make together, where to find a bezoar, and the difference between monkshood and wolfsbane. Harry knows none of them; Hermione's hand is in the air for all three. The answers: the Draught of Living Death; a goat's stomach; and none, because monkshood and wolfsbane are the same plant, also called aconite. Snape takes a point from Gryffindor for Harry's cheek.
*One line:* "I can teach you how to bottle fame, brew glory, even stopper death."
(Film note: the famous "no foolish wand-waving or silly incantations" is the film's wording; the book's line is quieter.)

**Why it fits:** two things. First, the speech is precise and controlled: short clauses, strong verbs, not a single "moreover". Second, the third question is a lesson in substitution: monkshood, wolfsbane and aconite are three names for one thing. A good essay refers to its subject by different names — "the ban", "this measure", "such a policy" — and the reader follows without a visible linker. The Whisper repeats the same twelve phrases because it has only one name for everything.

**Discussion (B1–B2):**
1. Snape's speech is short and strong. Find the verbs. What happens to the speech if you add "Firstly", "Moreover" and "In conclusion"?
2. Monkshood, wolfsbane, aconite: three names, one plant. In an essay about banning cars, what three names could you use for "the ban"?
3. Harry is embarrassed because he does not know the answers. Is fear a good teacher? What would help a nervous student more?

**IELTS Task 2 prompt (PROBLEM):** "Many secondary school students say they feel too anxious to speak in class, even when they know the answer. What are the causes of this problem? What could teachers and schools do to address it?"
Decode first: at least two causes with their mechanism, and each solution answers a named cause.

**2-minute warm-up — "Three names":** give her a noun phrase from the prompt ("student anxiety in class"). She writes three ways to refer to it without repeating it (a pronoun phrase, a nominalisation, a substitution such as "this reluctance"). Then she writes two sentences where the new idea of the first becomes the known idea of the second.

## Midwinter Tournament (optional event after chapter 5) · Examination Hall

**App skill:** working under a clock — a timed Duelling Hall run, one prompt cast at two minutes per key, and a full 40-minute essay. No band, only a token: proof that her own words come when the clock runs.

**Scene:** *Harry Potter and the Goblet of Fire* (book 4), chapter 16, "The Goblet of Fire".
Dumbledore draws an Age Line around the Goblet: only students of seventeen can enter. Fred and George take an Ageing Potion to cheat it and are thrown back with long white beards. On Halloween the Goblet gives out the three champions' names — Krum, Delacour, Diggory — and then, impossibly, a fourth: Harry's. The hall goes silent.
(Film note: in the film Dumbledore shakes Harry and shouts; in the book he asks "calmly".)

**Why it fits:** a tournament with three tasks, strict rules, a clock, and the lesson that a shortcut (the Ageing Potion) gets you a beard, not an entry. The Midwinter Tournament is the app's Age Line: open only to students with a clasp on their spellbook, because speed is useless before the method is yours.

**Discussion (B1–B2):**
1. Fred and George try to cheat the Age Line. Why does Dumbledore's rule exist, and was it fair to seventeen-year-olds too?
2. Harry did not choose to enter but must compete. Have you ever faced a test you did not choose? What helped?
3. A tournament with a clock: does time pressure make you write worse, or just faster? Give an example from your own tests.

**IELTS Task 2 prompt (OPINION):** "Governments should set a minimum age for dangerous sports and competitions, such as boxing and motor racing, regardless of how talented a young person is. To what extent do you agree or disagree?"
Decode first: a claim with two parts (minimum age; regardless of talent) — her position must cover both.

**2-minute warm-up — "Two minutes a key":** set a two-minute timer. She fills the Core Topic and Facet A for the prompt aloud, no writing. Stop when it rings, whatever is on the table. Then ask: what did you reach for first, the issue or an example? Her answer tells you where the Duelling Hall run will stall.

## Chapter 6 — The Library and the Newsroom · Long Library

**App skill:** collocation (the word and its partner), register (the Locked Stacks: contractions, "kids", clichés), and the six error families (article, plural, agreement, punctuation, word form, spelling). The Lantern exposes the Gossip Quill with a photo, a time and a name.

**Scene:** *Harry Potter and the Goblet of Fire* (book 4), chapter 18, "The Weighing of the Wands".
Before the champions' wands are checked, the journalist Rita Skeeter pulls Harry into a broom cupboard for an "interview". Her Quick-Quotes Quill writes by itself — and writes things Harry never said: tears, a tragic past, a brave young hero. The questions are leading, the answers are invented, and the article appears in the *Daily Prophet* the next day as if it were fact.
(Hermione's answer comes later: in book 5, chapter 25, "The Beetle at Bay", she makes Rita write Harry's true story for *The Quibbler*, with his name on it.)

**Why it fits:** the Quick-Quotes Quill is the Gossip Quill's ancestor. It produces fluent, emotional prose with no source, no example and no accountability — "some say", "tears fill his eyes", "a hot topic". The *Quillmoor Lantern*'s answer in chapter 6 is Hermione's answer in book 5: the same story told with names, a photograph and a time. Precision beats fluency.

**Discussion (B1–B2):**
1. The Quick-Quotes Quill writes things Harry never said. What makes a sentence "evidence" and what makes it "fog"? Give one example of each.
2. Rita's article is well written and completely untrue. Which matters more to a reader, style or truth? Which matters more to an examiner?
3. Hermione later gets the true story printed with Harry's name on it. Why does a name change how much we believe a story?

**IELTS Task 2 prompt (TWOPART):** "Many people now get their news from social media rather than from newspapers or television. Why has this happened? Is it a positive or a negative development?"
Decode first: two questions, one body paragraph each, and the verdict (positive or negative) in paragraph one as well as the conclusion.

**2-minute warm-up — "The Locked Stacks":** read her four sentences from a Quick-Quotes-style article (one contraction, one "kids", one cliché, one "research shows"). She names what is wrong with each in one word (register, register, cliché, no source) and fixes one of them aloud with a formal partner word.

## Chapter 7 — The Grand Examination · Examination Hall

**App skill:** the four examiners in plain words (TR, CC, LR, GRA as gates), the question-type playbooks, the Exemplar Archive (read for shape, never to copy), the 5/30/5 protocol, three mocks, and one essay marked at 7.5 or above by LevelUp.

**Scene:** *Harry Potter and the Order of the Phoenix* (book 5), chapter 31, "O.W.L.s".
The fifth-years sit their Ordinary Wizarding Levels: a written paper and a practical for each subject, marked not by their own teachers but by Ministry examiners such as Professor Marchbanks and Professor Tofty. Hermione panics over a mistranslated rune; Harry's practicals go well. Then, during the History of Magic paper, he falls asleep and sees Voldemort's vision of Sirius, which sends him to the Ministry.
(Film note: the film shows almost none of the exams; use the book.)

**Why it fits:** external examiners, fixed criteria, one chance under a clock — and the two mistakes the chapter shows. Hermione loses marks to a small, checkable error she could have caught with five minutes of proofreading. Harry loses the paper entirely by letting something else take his attention. The Grand Examination's protocol (five minutes to decode, thirty to write, five to check) exists to stop both.

**Discussion (B1–B2):**
1. The O.W.L.s are marked by outside examiners, not the students' own teachers. Why? Is that better or worse for the students?
2. Hermione worries about one small mistake. What is one small, fixable error you make in every essay, and how would you check for it in five minutes?
3. Harry loses his last exam because his mind is somewhere else. What takes your attention away in a test, and what is your plan for it?

**IELTS Task 2 prompt (DISCUSS):** "Some people believe that a single final examination is the fairest way to assess what students have learned. Others think that continuous assessment through coursework and projects gives a truer picture. Discuss both views and give your own opinion."
Decode first: both views with mechanism and example; verdict in paragraph one and the conclusion.

**2-minute warm-up — "The examiner's table":** she is Professor Penhallow. In two minutes she tells you, in plain words and without notes, what each of the four examiners (North, Brigg, Liang, Weaver) needs to see for a 7. If she says "good vocabulary" or "no mistakes", ask her for the gate wording from the app: "precise words with their partners", "the majority of sentences error-free".

## Using this page with 1:1 online tutees on iPad

- **Keep the scene off the screen.** The app is open on her iPad; the Harry Potter scene is only in your voice. Do not share a page from the book or a film clip in the call — the IP line is "discussed in class, never inside the app or a shared file".
- **Order of a 50-minute lesson:** 2-minute warm-up (above) → 3 minutes of discussion, one question is enough → she opens the chapter in the app and reads the pages aloud, tapping keywords → missions → the writing task in the Scriptorium. The scene is the hook, not the content.
- **If she has not read the books,** tell the scene in sixty seconds in your own words; the summaries above are written to be read aloud at B1. Ask whether she has seen the film; if so, say which version you are using. The HBO series (season 1 from 25 December 2026) will give the tutees a third version — check this page against it when it airs.
- **Discussion by voice, writing by hand.** Ask the discussion question, let her answer aloud, then have her type the one-sentence position into the Scriptorium before the missions. Speaking first lowers the fear; typing second gives you something to quote back.
- **Only one Harry Potter line per lesson.** Quote one short line, in your voice, and move on. The tutees are preparing for an exam in which "In today's modern world" and "a double-edged sword" cost marks; Rowling's prose is good, but her sentences are not their sentences either.
- **The Sorting result is a strength, not a label.** When a tutee is sorted (chapter 1), say what the house's examiner counts and name her weakest criterion as the one the story will train next. The app's `challengeLine` does the same; echo it rather than contradict it.
- **Owl post and the Staff Room.** Assign the chapter's prompt as owl post after the lesson; read her essay in the Staff Room before the next call and open with one quoted sentence of hers, as Penhallow would.
